Read Between the Waves

Literary analysis is the practice of making a clear, arguable claim about a text and backing it up with evidence from that text. This guide walks you through the core elements, shows the method on a real novel, and lets you test yourself along the way.

What literary analysis is

A plot summary tells a reader what happens. A literary analysis explains how and why the text makes its effects. The difference is the claim: an analysis takes a position that another reasonable reader could question, then shows how specific words, scenes, and structures support it.

Most strong analytical paragraphs follow a simple pattern. State the point, quote or describe the evidence, then explain how that evidence supports the point. Many teachers call this claim, evidence, analysis.

Weak thesis

The Old Man and the Sea is about an old fisherman who catches a big fish.

Stronger thesis

In The Old Man and the Sea, Hemingway suggests that dignity matters more than victory, because Santiago’s worth is shown in how he endures defeat rather than in the marlin he brings home.

Key elements to look for

Open any element to see a short definition and what to look for when you read. You do not need every element in every essay. Choose the ones that serve your claim.

Theme

The central idea or message a text communicates about life, people, or society. A theme is usually a full statement, not a single word. “Love” is a topic; “love can survive loss but not indifference” is a theme.

Character

The people, animals, or beings who act in a story. Look at what characters say, what they do, how others react to them, and how they change. Static characters stay the same; dynamic characters change in response to events.

Setting

The time and place of a story, including its social and emotional atmosphere. Setting often shapes character and conflict. A remote village and a crowded city can produce very different pressures on the same person.

Plot structure

The sequence of events, usually organized as exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution. Analysts often ask why a writer delays the climax, or why a resolution feels incomplete.

Point of view

The perspective from which the story is told. In first person, the narrator uses “I” and can only report what they know or perceive. In third-person limited, the narrator follows one character’s thoughts. In third-person omniscient, the narrator knows all characters’ thoughts.

Symbolism

When a concrete object, place, or image represents a larger idea. A symbol earns its meaning through repetition and context. Ask whether the text keeps returning to the object and whether the meaning changes over time.

Motif

A recurring image, phrase, or pattern that develops a theme. Unlike a single symbol, a motif often appears in many forms throughout the text. Tracking motifs helps you see how a writer builds meaning gradually.

Irony

A gap between expectation and reality. Verbal irony means saying one thing and meaning another. Situational irony means an outcome contradicts what was expected. Dramatic irony means the audience knows something a character does not.

Tone and mood

Tone is the writer’s attitude toward the subject, such as tender, mocking, or resigned. Mood is the feeling the text creates in the reader. Good analysis connects specific word choices and sentence rhythms to the tone and mood they produce.

Worked example: The Old Man and the Sea

Ernest Hemingway, 1952. A novella about Santiago, an aging Cuban fisherman, and the marlin he hooks far out in the Gulf Stream.

Hemingway wrote about his method through what he called the iceberg theory: most of the meaning should sit beneath the surface, supported by the parts a writer chooses to show. An analysis can test that idea by asking what the story leaves unsaid.

Step 1: Find a question

Santiago has gone 84 days without catching a fish, and the boy Manolin has been forced to leave him. A good question is: what does Santiago gain from the struggle, if the marlin is lost to sharks by the end?

Step 2: Gather evidence

Pride and routine

Santiago talks to himself, to the fish, and to the sea. His repeated lines about being “a strange old man” show how he sees himself.

The marlin

He calls the fish his brother and respects it. Respect, not conquest, shapes the way he fights.

Defeat

When the sharks strip the marlin, Santiago measures his loss not in money but in what he has kept of himself.

Step 3: Explain the connection

Claim: Hemingway shows that dignity is measured by how a person endures, not by what the person keeps.

Evidence: The sharks take almost everything from Santiago, yet he returns to shore with the marlin’s skeleton and still feels that he has been defeated only in part.

Analysis: The skeleton functions as a symbol of what remains after loss. Santiago’s exhaustion does not erase his skill or his sense of who he is, which supports the claim that his worth is located in his conduct.

Notice the structure of this paragraph. The analysis does not stop at saying what happens. It connects a specific image to a larger idea, then explains why that idea matters for the reader.
